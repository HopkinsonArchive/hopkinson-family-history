**Lowell Herbert Hopkinson** was born in Bradford, Massachusetts, on June 15, 1836. Raised principally in the household of his maternal grandparents, [[people/Uriah Hopkinson|Uriah Hopkinson]] and [[Inez Lucy Chase|Inez “Lucy” Chase]], he became a shoemaker, husband, father, Union soldier, prisoner of war, Groveland constable, and member of the Grand Army of the Republic.

His Civil War service was unusually severe. Lowell was wounded at Second Bull Run during his first enlistment, returned to the army, was captured at Cold Harbor, and spent approximately six months in Confederate captivity. Decades later he developed progressive disability described in federal records as paralysis. By 1896 he was bedridden, helpless, and dependent upon a small pension and assistance from friends.

> [!important] Corrected military identification
> Lowell served in **Company H, 11th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry**, followed by **Company A, 59th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry**, which was consolidated into the **57th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry** near the end of the war.
>
> He did **not** serve in the 2nd Massachusetts Heavy Artillery. The earlier attribution to that regiment and to a wound at Winchester was incorrect.

## Parentage and childhood

Lowell’s surviving Bradford birth record identifies his mother as **Miss Ann Matilda Hopkinson** and does not name his father. Thirty-nine days before his birth, Ann and **William Pillsbury Bailey of West Newbury** filed a formal intention to marry. No completed marriage between them has been found.

The archive therefore treats William as Lowell’s biological father by **strong circumstantial inference**, not direct proof. The timing and public nature of the marriage intention are compelling, and no competing candidate has emerged, but no surviving paternity acknowledgment, court judgment, guardianship paper, or birth record explicitly names William as Lowell’s father.

Lowell retained his mother’s surname. By the 1850 census, at age thirteen, he lived with Ann’s parents, Uriah and Lucy Hopkinson. Ann had married John R. Estabrooks in 1839 and maintained a separate household. The evidence supports the conclusion that Uriah and Lucy were Lowell’s principal parental household, but it does not establish precisely when he entered their home or justify describing Ann as having abandoned him.

## Shoemaker, husband, and father

Lowell became a **shoemaker**, an occupation recorded in both military enlistments and characteristic of the Merrimack Valley economy. Ann was later recorded as a shoe stitcher, while other relatives worked as cordwainers or in shoe factories. The records demonstrate a family connection to shoe production but do not identify who taught Lowell the trade.

Lowell married **Margaret Metcalf**. Their reported children include Clarence E., Lewis Brown, Annie Low, and [[Harry Francis Hopkinson|Harry Francis Hopkinson]], who continues the archive’s direct line. The marriage record and complete child list still require presentation from original vital records.

## First enlistment: 11th Massachusetts Infantry

The Massachusetts state roster records Lowell as a Groveland resident, age twenty-four, and a shoemaker when he enlisted and mustered into **Company H, 11th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry**, on June 13, 1861. He entered as a **private**.

The regiment participated in First Bull Run, the Peninsula Campaign, and Second Bull Run. Lowell’s presence at Second Bull Run is directly documented. His presence in every earlier action is strongly supported by his continuous service but remains subject to company-level absence, hospital, or detachment records.

### Wounded at Second Bull Run

On August 29, 1862, Lowell was wounded at **Second Bull Run**, also called Second Manassas. The later congressional pension report described a severe wound to his **right elbow**. Pension material summarized in that same report referred to a **gunshot wound of the left arm**, producing a conflict that the complete pension jacket may resolve.

Lowell was discharged for wounds at Philadelphia on October 25, 1862. Although he had enlisted as a private, the state roster records his discharge **as a corporal**.

## Second enlistment: 59th Massachusetts Infantry

Despite his earlier wound, Lowell enlisted again on November 14, 1863, and mustered into **Company A, 59th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry**, on December 5 as a **private**. The 59th served with the IX Corps during the Overland Campaign.

### Capture at Cold Harbor

Lowell was captured at **Cold Harbor, Virginia, on June 3, 1864**, during the major Union assault. His official roster gives December 14, 1864, as his exchange date; the congressional report gives December 10 as his parole date. Parole and formal exchange were distinct processes, so these dates are not necessarily contradictory.

Affidavits submitted for Lowell’s later special pension stated that he was confined in Libby Prison, Pemberton Prison, Andersonville, and Florence Stockade. The congressional report also states that he was treated for **chronic diarrhea and pleurisy contracted while a prisoner of war**. The prison sequence should eventually be tested against original prisoner registers and transfer records.

### Promotion and final service

The state roster documents Lowell’s transfer to the **57th Massachusetts Infantry on June 1, 1865, as a sergeant**. This resulted from consolidation of the depleted 59th into the 57th and was not a third enlistment.

| Service | Entry rank | Final documented rank |
|---|---|---|
| 11th Massachusetts, 1861–1862 | Private | Corporal |
| 59th/57th Massachusetts, 1863–1865 | Private | Sergeant |

A surviving privately produced service memorial identifies him as **Color Sergeant Lowell H. Hopkinson** and reportedly depicts crossed flags and a sergeant’s chevron. That appointment is plausible, but no original order, muster-roll annotation, or company book yet confirms the specific title.

## Groveland constable and GAR member

Federal papers concerning the contested congressional election of E. Moody Boynton and George B. Loring identify **“Lowell H. Hopkinson, one of the constables in the town of Groveland”** in connection with the November 1878 election. This establishes local civil service more than a decade after the war, although his complete term and duties require Groveland town records.

Lowell also belonged to **Benjamin Stone Jr. Post No. 68 of the Grand Army of the Republic**. His name appears in the post’s 1892 historical souvenir. The GAR provided commemoration, fellowship, advocacy, and mutual assistance to Union veterans and their families.

## Progressive disability and pension campaign

Lowell initially received a federal pension for his wartime arm wound and kidney disease. The congressional report summarized this history:

| Date or period | Monthly pension |
|---|---:|
| Initial award | $5 |
| September 17, 1890 | $8 |
| November 12, 1895 | $12 |
| Special act approved June 20, 1898 | $30 |

By December 1896 Lowell was described as **“paralyzed and bedridden,”** entirely helpless, and unable to care for himself. The report stated that he depended upon the charity of friends apart from his small pension. A bill initially proposed $50 per month; the Senate recommended $30, and the private act was approved June 20, 1898.

The report proves complete disability by late 1896 but does not reveal the precise onset, anatomical pattern, diagnosis, or progression.

## Margaret and daily care

The published report does not name Lowell’s caregiver. Margaret was alive during his disabled years, and the couple remained associated with their Groveland home. It is therefore a **strong practical inference** that she provided much of his continuous household care.

A helpless, bedridden adult would likely have needed help with repositioning, washing, dressing, feeding, toileting, bedding, transfers, medicine, food, fuel, and nighttime supervision. The evidence does not show that Margaret performed every task alone. The report’s reference to friends, Lowell’s GAR network, adult children, and nearby Hopkinson households make a wider support system plausible.

Dr. Louis Augustus Woodbury photographed **“Lowell H. Hopkinson’s house”** on August 13, 1899. His catalogue sequence places Thomas Hopkinson, Paul Hopkinson, Lowell, and George Edwin and Rufus Hopkinson within the same survey run, suggesting geographic proximity. Catalogue order alone does not prove exact adjacency or caregiving.

No examined source names Margaret, Harry, Lewis, or another relative as Lowell’s attendant. Specific caregiving assignments remain unresolved pending the complete pension file, 1900 census, town records, or family correspondence.

## Could Lowell have had a motor-neuron disease?

No surviving record examined so far diagnoses Lowell with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, progressive muscular atrophy, primary lateral sclerosis, or another motor-neuron disease. **He should not be presented as a confirmed ALS patient.**

A slowly progressive motor-neuron disorder remains a reasonable retrospective hypothesis because later records describe generalized paralysis rather than disability confined to the wounded arm; he became helpless and bedridden; the course may have developed over years; and he survived about eight years after being documented as bedridden. “Paralysis” could also have described profound weakness rather than complete loss of movement.

Alternative explanations include stroke, spinal-cord disease, cervical myelopathy, post-infectious disease, multiple sclerosis, neurosyphilis, peripheral neuropathy, muscular disease, or another chronic neurological disorder.

Severe trauma, imprisonment, infection, malnutrition, and prolonged physiological stress are important parts of Lowell’s medical history. Modern ALS research examines glutamate excitotoxicity, calcium dysregulation, mitochondrial injury, oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, proteostasis failure, and nuclear-envelope damage. Those mechanisms cannot diagnose Lowell retrospectively or establish wartime stress as the direct cause.

> Lowell may have suffered from a slowly progressive motor-neuron disease or another chronic neurological disorder. ALS-spectrum disease is plausible, but the evidence presently supports a hypothesis rather than a diagnosis.

The complete federal pension file may contain examinations describing muscular wasting, reflexes, sensation, speech, swallowing, contractures, and the sequence of functional loss.

## Home, death, and burial

Woodbury’s 1899 glass-plate photograph shows a two-story light-colored dwelling, an adjacent wooden structure, and a picket fence. It records the setting of Lowell’s profound disability, but its catalogue title does not by itself prove legal ownership. See [Lowell H. Hopkinson’s house, Historic New England](https://www.historicnewengland.org/explore/collections-access/gusn/394359).

Lowell reportedly died in Groveland on **May 2, 1904**, aged sixty-seven, and was buried in Riverview Cemetery. The original death record, cemetery register, and obituary have not yet been examined. Margaret survived him and reportedly died in 1917; whether she filed a widow’s pension claim remains unresolved.

## Evidence assessment

### Confirmed

- Ann Matilda’s motherhood, Lowell’s Bradford birth, and his 1850 residence with Uriah and Lucy.
- Occupation as a shoemaker.
- Service in the 11th, 59th, and 57th Massachusetts Infantry.
- Wounding at Second Bull Run and discharge as a corporal.
- Capture at Cold Harbor, exchange, and later transfer as a sergeant.
- Prison-related treatment for chronic diarrhea and pleurisy.
- Service as a Groveland constable in 1878 and GAR membership by 1892.
- Pension increases, complete helplessness by December 1896, and the 1898 special act.
- Identification of his Groveland house in an 1899 photograph.

### Strong inference

- William Pillsbury Bailey was Lowell’s biological father.
- Uriah and Lucy were Lowell’s principal parental household.
- Margaret provided much of his continuous household care.
- Relatives, friends, neighbors, or GAR comrades helped with some aspects of care.

### Unverified or unresolved

- Specific appointment as color sergeant.
- Exact complete child list and prison-transfer sequence.
- Which arm was wounded and pensioned.
- Onset, progression, diagnosis, and cause of his paralysis.
- Names and roles of daily caregivers and the 1900 household.
- Legal ownership and exact location of the photographed house.
- Original confirmation of death date, burial, cause of death, and Margaret’s widow claim.
