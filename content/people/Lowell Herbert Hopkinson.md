> [!profile] Lowell Herbert Hopkinson (1836–reported 1904)
> **Born:** June 15, 1836 · Bradford, Massachusetts  
> **Died:** Reported May 2, 1904 · Groveland, Massachusetts  
> **Occupation:** Shoemaker  
> **Public service:** Groveland constable  
> **Military service:** 11th, 59th, and 57th Massachusetts Infantry · corporal · sergeant · prisoner of war  
> **Evidence status:** Mixed

**Lowell Herbert Hopkinson** was born in Bradford, Massachusetts, on June 15, 1836, to [[people/Ann Matilda Hopkinson|Ann Matilda Hopkinson]]. His birth record does not name a father. Ann's marriage intention with [[people/William Pillsbury Bailey|William Pillsbury Bailey]], filed thirty-nine days before Lowell's birth, makes William the strongly inferred biological father, but direct proof has not been found.[^birth-parentage]

By 1850 Lowell lived in Groveland with his maternal grandparents, [[people/Uriah Hopkinson|Uriah Hopkinson]] and [[people/Inez Lucy Chase|Inez “Lucy” Chase]]. He became a shoemaker, married [[people/Margaret Metcalf|Margaret Metcalf]], and raised a family in Groveland. During the Civil War he was wounded at Second Bull Run, reenlisted, was captured at Cold Harbor, survived approximately six months in Confederate captivity, and completed his service as a sergeant.[^census-1850][^roster-11th][^roster-59th]

Lowell later served as a Groveland constable and belonged to a local Grand Army of the Republic post. By December 1896 a congressional report described him as paralyzed, bedridden, entirely helpless, and unable to care for himself. The cause and progression of that disability remain unknown.[^constable][^gar][^pension-report]

## Family and relationships

Lowell's maternal relationship is direct: his Bradford birth record names **Miss Ann Matilda Hopkinson** as his mother. It does not name his father. Ann and William Pillsbury Bailey of West Newbury filed an intention to marry on May 7, 1836, while Ann was pregnant; Lowell was born on June 15. No completed marriage record or document explicitly identifying William as Lowell's father has been found in the sources examined. William is therefore treated as Lowell's biological father by **strong inference**, not as a confirmed relationship.[^birth-parentage]

Lowell retained the Hopkinson surname. In the 1850 census, when he was thirteen, he was enumerated in the Groveland household of his maternal grandparents Uriah and Lucy Hopkinson. Ann had married John R. Estabrooks in 1839 and maintained a separate household. The census confirms Lowell's residence with his grandparents in 1850, but not when that arrangement began or its legal character.[^census-1850]

Lowell married Margaret Metcalf. The following child list is preserved from the archive's accumulated family research, but the complete set of original birth and parentage records has not yet been assembled. Harry Francis's place in the direct line is established more securely than the complete sibling group.[^family]

## Life and historical context

### Birth and childhood

Lowell was born into an unresolved parental situation. The town record establishes Ann Matilda's maternity, while the nearby marriage intention supplies the principal evidence for William Pillsbury Bailey's probable paternity. Because no completed marriage or paternity record has been located, the archive preserves both the documentary limit and the working conclusion.[^birth-parentage]

Bradford's northern section became Groveland in 1850, the same year the federal census placed Lowell in the household of Uriah and Lucy Hopkinson. He was then thirteen. That household connection helps explain the Hopkinson identity he retained, but it does not establish whether his grandparents had raised him continuously from infancy.[^census-1850]

### Marriage and household

Lowell and Margaret established their family in Groveland. The currently reported four-child list requires systematic checking against town birth registers, censuses, marriage records, and death records. The archive does not yet claim that the list is complete.[^family]

An 1899 glass-plate photograph cataloged by Historic New England as **“Lowell H. Hopkinson's house, Groveland”** preserves the setting associated with his final years. The catalog title establishes the contemporary association; it does not by itself prove legal ownership or identify the property's street address.[^house-photo]

## Occupation and economic life

Both Massachusetts military roster entries identify Lowell as a **shoemaker** and a resident of Groveland. This is direct evidence of his occupation in 1861 and again in 1863. The records reviewed do not yet establish his employer, workshop, specialty, property holdings, or the full duration of his trade.[^roster-11th][^roster-59th]

## Military and public service

### First enlistment: 11th Massachusetts Infantry

The Massachusetts roster records Lowell as a twenty-four-year-old Groveland shoemaker when he enlisted and mustered as a private in Company H, 11th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry, on June 13, 1861. A later congressional report gives April 20, 1861, as the beginning of this service. The reason for that date conflict remains unresolved; the detailed state roster is preferred for the formal enlistment and muster date.[^roster-11th][^pension-report]

On August 29, 1862, Lowell was wounded at Second Bull Run. The roster records the event without anatomical detail. The congressional report describes a severe wound of the **right elbow**, but its summary of Lowell's pension basis refers to a **gunshot wound of the left arm**. Neither side is silently selected here. He was discharged for wounds at Philadelphia on October 25, 1862, and the state roster records him at discharge as a corporal.[^roster-11th][^pension-report]

### Second enlistment: 59th and 57th Massachusetts Infantry

Lowell enlisted again on November 14, 1863, and mustered as a private in Company A, 59th Massachusetts Infantry, on December 5. He was captured at Cold Harbor on June 3, 1864. The roster gives December 14 as the date of exchange; the congressional report gives December 10 as the date of parole. Parole and formal exchange were distinct stages, so the two dates may describe different events.[^roster-59th][^pension-report]

Affidavits summarized by the Senate Committee on Pensions stated that Lowell passed through Libby Prison, Pemberton Prison, Andersonville, and Florence Stockade. The same report stated that he was treated for chronic diarrhea and pleurisy contracted while a prisoner. These details are confirmed as statements contained in the official report, but the full prison sequence still awaits comparison with Confederate prison registers, Union prisoner-of-war records, and Lowell's complete pension file.[^pension-report]

On June 1, 1865, Lowell transferred to Company A, 57th Massachusetts Infantry **as a sergeant**, and he completed service on July 30. The official rosters document corporal and sergeant; the more specific title **color sergeant**, painted on a surviving privately held service memorial, remains unverified by an appointment record.[^roster-59th][^memorial]

### Groveland constable and Grand Army of the Republic

Testimony printed in the federal contested-election case *E. Moody Boynton v. George B. Loring* identifies Lowell as “one of the constables in the town of Groveland” during the November 1878 election. This establishes the office, though not the dates of his full term or the complete scope of his duties.[^constable]

Lowell's name also appears in the 1892 historical souvenir of Benjamin Stone Jr. Post No. 68, Department of Massachusetts, Grand Army of the Republic. That contemporary membership list is preferred over later commercial accounts that associate him with a different post.[^gar]

## Disability, pension, and care

The Senate Committee on Pensions reported that Lowell received a pension for an arm wound and kidney disease. It described increases from &#36;5 per month to &#36;8 on September 17, 1890, and to &#36;12 on November 12, 1895.[^pension-report]

By December 1896 Lowell was described as **“paralyzed and bedridden,”** entirely helpless, and unable to care for himself. The report said that, beyond his small pension, he depended on the charity of friends. A bill proposed &#36;50 per month; the Senate recommended &#36;30, and a private act approved that amount on June 20, 1898.[^pension-report][^private-act]

The report does not identify when the paralysis began, whether “paralyzed” meant loss of movement or profound progressive weakness, or who performed particular caregiving tasks. Margaret was alive during these years and may have supplied much of the household care, but this remains an inference. The explicit reference to aid from friends shows that support extended beyond Lowell himself; it does not identify those friends or establish whether adult children, relatives, neighbors, paid attendants, or G.A.R. comrades assisted.[^pension-report]

### Motor-neuron disease as the leading interpretation

The cause of Lowell's disability is not recorded in the sources examined. On the accumulated evidence, the archive regards a **slowly progressive motor-neuron disease as the most likely diagnostic category**. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is the principal candidate within that category, partly because a later deceased direct-line descendant reportedly received a clinical ALS diagnosis after severe combat trauma. That family recurrence materially strengthens the interpretation, but it cannot by itself establish Lowell's specific diagnosis or prove a genetic or combat-related cause.[^family-medical]

ALS was described as a distinct neurological disease by Jean-Martin Charcot in the nineteenth century, before Lowell became bedridden, but diagnostic knowledge and terminology were not uniformly applied. A period description of “paralysis” could encompass several neurological or systemic disorders. Without a clinical history documenting the distribution and progression of weakness, upper- and lower-motor-neuron signs, sensation, swallowing, breathing, or cognition, ALS cannot be distinguished from stroke, spinal-cord disease, cervical myelopathy, neuropathy, infection, or other causes.[^als-history]

Modern research links ALS pathobiology with excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, disturbed protein homeostasis, and regulated cell-death pathways. Those mechanisms make the family's biological hypothesis intelligible, but they cannot establish that wartime trauma, imprisonment, infection, or stress caused Lowell's condition.[^als-review]

## Death and burial

Lowell is reported to have died in Groveland on May 2, 1904, at age sixty-seven, and to have been buried in Riverview Cemetery. These details currently rest on later compiled or memorial sources rather than an original death return, cemetery register, gravestone transcription made for this project, or contemporary obituary. The date is therefore left out of the machine-readable `death_date` field and classified as **Unverified**. His cause of death, cemetery section, and plot remain unresolved.[^death-burial]

## Evidence assessment

### Confirmed

- Lowell was born in Bradford on June 15, 1836, and his birth record names Ann Matilda Hopkinson as his mother.[^birth-parentage]
- He lived with Uriah and Lucy Hopkinson in Groveland in 1850.[^census-1850]
- He was a shoemaker and served in the 11th, 59th, and 57th Massachusetts Infantry.[^roster-11th][^roster-59th]
- He was wounded at Second Bull Run, discharged as a corporal, captured at Cold Harbor, and later transferred as a sergeant.[^roster-11th][^roster-59th]
- A federal report records prison-related chronic diarrhea and pleurisy, his pension history, and his complete disability by December 1896.[^pension-report]
- He served as a Groveland constable in 1878 and was listed with Benjamin Stone Jr. Post No. 68 in 1892.[^constable][^gar]
- Historic New England catalogs an August 13, 1899 photograph as Lowell H. Hopkinson's house in Groveland.[^house-photo]

### Strong inference

- William Pillsbury Bailey was Lowell's biological father. The inference rests principally on his marriage intention with Ann Matilda thirty-nine days before Lowell's birth; no record yet names him directly as the father.[^birth-parentage]
- Uriah and Lucy supplied Lowell's principal childhood household. Their 1850 household is confirmed, but the starting date and continuity of the arrangement are unknown.[^census-1850]

### Unverified

- The reported death date of May 2, 1904 and burial in Riverview Cemetery await original or contemporary confirmation.[^death-burial]
- The reported full child list requires original vital and household records; Harry Francis's direct-line relationship is established more securely than the full sibling group.[^family]
- “Color sergeant” appears on a surviving service memorial, but no appointment record has yet been examined.[^memorial]
- A slowly progressive motor-neuron disease is the archive's most likely interpretation of Lowell's paralysis; ALS is the principal candidate, but the specific diagnosis remains unverified.[^family-medical][^als-history]

### Unresolved

- Direct proof of William Pillsbury Bailey's paternity and evidence of a completed marriage to Ann Matilda.
- The source of the June 13 versus June 15 birth-date discrepancy.
- The exact date and place of Lowell and Margaret's marriage and complete original-record documentation for their children.
- Why the state roster and congressional report give different first-enlistment dates.
- Whether the right-elbow and left-arm wound descriptions refer to the same injury, a clerical error, or more than one condition.
- Lowell's exact route through Confederate prisons and the underlying records for each transfer.
- The onset, progression, anatomical pattern, and cause of his paralysis.
- The identities and roles of Margaret, relatives, friends, or paid attendants in his daily care.
- The legal ownership, street address, and subsequent history of the photographed house.
- Original confirmation of Lowell's death, cause of death, burial location, cemetery section, and plot.

## Research priorities

1. Obtain Lowell's complete federal pension and medical files, including surgeon's certificates, affidavits, and Margaret's possible widow claim.
2. Obtain the original Groveland death return, contemporary obituary, Riverview Cemetery register entry, and gravestone documentation.
3. Search hospital, prisoner-of-war, parole, and exchange records to reconstruct the wound history, prison sequence, illnesses, and dates.
4. Locate an order, muster roll, descriptive book, or other primary record that confirms or disproves the color-sergeant title.
5. Assemble original marriage and birth records for Margaret and every reported child, then reconcile the household in the 1860–1900 censuses.
6. Locate deeds, maps, tax records, and directories for the house photographed in 1899.
7. Seek additional direct evidence concerning William Pillsbury Bailey's paternity and the outcome of the 1836 marriage intention.
