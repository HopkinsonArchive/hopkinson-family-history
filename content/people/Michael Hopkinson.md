## Summary

Michael Hopkinson was an early settler of Rowley, Massachusetts. He was first documented in Boston in 1638, where he was employed by Jacob Eliot and admitted to the Boston church. In 1639 he transferred to the newly gathered church at Rowley. He received a house lot there and became a Massachusetts Bay freeman on May 13, 1640.

Michael’s estate included a house, barn, orchard, cultivated land, meadow, livestock, books, arms, silver, weaving equipment, and an interest in a boat. His looms and specialized weaving tools strongly indicate that he worked as a weaver or textile producer while also farming.

He was buried at Rowley on February 28, 1648/49. His youngest documented son, Caleb Hopkinson, had been born only days earlier and became the next ancestor in the direct Hopkinson line.

## Known Record

### Boston

Michael was living in Boston by December 1638. A transcription of the Boston church record states that he was employed by Jacob Eliot when he was admitted to the church on December 6, 1638.

On November 24, 1639, Michael was dismissed from the Boston church:

> “to the gathering of a church at Rowley”

This indicates that Michael joined the congregation being organized for the new settlement at Rowley.

His precise arrival date, immigration ship, and port of departure have not been identified.

### Rowley

Michael became one of Rowley’s early settlers and proprietors. He received a house lot of approximately 1½ acres, apparently located along what became Bradford Street.

He was admitted as a freeman of the Massachusetts Bay Colony on May 13, 1640.

Freemanship was a formal political status. At this period it ordinarily required acceptance within the colony’s Puritan church system and allowed a man to vote in colonial elections and participate more fully in public affairs.

No record has yet been found showing Michael serving as a selectman, constable, deputy, or other town officer.

## Occupation

No surviving record examined so far explicitly calls Michael a weaver. His estate inventory, however, contained an extensive collection of textile equipment:

- A pair of looms
- A shuttle
- A weaving temple
- Warping equipment
- Reeds
- Several slays
- Multiple wheels
- Cotton wool
- Yarn
- Cloth and textile materials

A weaving temple maintained the width of cloth while it was being woven. Reeds, slays, shuttles, and warping equipment were specialized tools used with a loom.

The quantity and range of equipment are substantially greater than ordinary household spinning tools.

**Strong inference:** Michael worked as a professional or semi-professional weaver or textile producer.

His land, crops, livestock, and agricultural tools also show that he operated a farm.

## Property and Estate

Michael’s estate was valued at **£116 19s. 8d.** before the deduction of debts.

The inventory included:

### Real property

- House and barn
- Garden and orchard
- Yards and adjoining swamp
- Approximately 2½ acres of cultivated land at Batchelder Plain
- Approximately five acres in the Bradford lots
- Approximately eight acres of meadow
- Approximately one acre of upland
- Common grazing rights

### Livestock

- Three cows
- Two three-year-old heifers
- Two steers
- One yearling
- Three calves

### Household and working property

- Looms and weaving implements
- Cotton wool, yarn, and cloth
- Agricultural and woodworking tools
- Beds and household furnishings
- Cooking vessels
- Books
- Silver
- Armor and gunpowder
- A financial interest in a boat

The type of boat and Michael’s use of it are not known. Its recorded value, **£10 9s.**, made it one of the more valuable movable interests in his estate.

The inventory depicts a productive household combining farming, livestock raising, textile production, and possibly some form of waterborne transport or commerce.

## Later Land Right

A later Rowley division assigned 67 acres to Richard Swan:

> “in right of Michael Hopkinson”

Richard Swan subsequently became the third husband of Michael’s widow, Ann.

This was apparently a later realization of proprietary rights originally belonging to Michael. It should not be interpreted as proof that Michael personally occupied or cultivated all 67 acres during his lifetime.

## Marriage

Michael’s documented wife was named **Ann**. Her maiden name and parents have not been established.

Following Michael’s death, Ann married:

1. John Trumble, in 1650
2. Richard Swan, on March 1, 1658/59

Ann stated in a 1675/76 deposition that she was approximately 60 years old, suggesting that she was born around 1615 or 1616.

## The Kildwick Identification

A parish record from Kildwick, Yorkshire, documents a Michael Hopkinson marrying Anna Brigge on December 2, 1624.

Numerous online family trees identify that couple as the later Rowley immigrants and assign Michael parents named Daniel Hopkinson and Susan Emot. This identification has not been proved.

The age given by Michael’s Massachusetts widow creates a substantial conflict. If she was approximately 60 in 1675/76, she would have been only about eight or nine years old at the time of the Kildwick marriage.

Possible explanations include:

- The Kildwick Michael and the Rowley Michael were different men.
- Michael married Anna Brigge first and later married a younger woman also named Ann.
- The Massachusetts Ann’s reported age was incorrect by approximately eleven years.

No record currently establishes that Michael had two wives or directly connects the Kildwick man to Boston and Rowley.

**Project conclusion:** Michael’s wife remains Ann, maiden name unknown. The Kildwick marriage and the proposed Daniel Hopkinson–Susan Emot ancestry are unverified leads and are not presently part of the documented family line.

## Disputed Children

Some online trees assign Michael and Ann several older children born in England, including:

- Johannes Hopkinson
- Richardus Hopkinson
- Susanna Hopkinson
- Mary Hopkinson
- Ann Hopkinson
- Another Michael Hopkinson

Other claims identify Ann Wood, Mary Grant, and Susanna Todd as Michael’s daughters.

A 1657 court division described the combined household as containing:

- Four children of Michael Hopkinson
- Five children of John Trumble by his earlier wife
- Two children of John and Ann together

Michael’s four surviving children were specifically identified as Jonathan, Jeremiah, John, and Caleb.

The court did not identify Ann Wood, Mary Grant, Susanna Todd, or any other surviving daughters as Michael’s children.

**Project conclusion:** The supposed older children and daughters are unverified and should not be included as Michael’s children without additional evidence.

## Death and Burial

Michael’s precise date and cause of death are unknown. He was buried at Rowley on February 28, 1648/49.

Because seventeenth-century English records treated March as the first numbered month and the legal year did not change until March 25, the original date appeared as February 28, 1648. In the modern calendar, this occurred in 1649.

The estate inventory was apparently completed shortly after his death, around March 10, 1649. One transcription gives an inconsistent year in the heading, but the accompanying signature and sequence support 1649.

## Delayed Estate Administration

Although an inventory was prepared after Michael’s death, Ann did not promptly complete formal administration of the estate.

In 1657, her second husband, John Trumble, petitioned the General Court to remit a fine imposed because Michael’s estate had not been administered. The petition stated that:

- Michael died in 1648 under the old calendar
- An inventory had been taken
- The estate had not been alienated
- The property remained available for Ann and the children

The request was granted on the condition that the inventory be presented to the county court and the estate arranged for the widow and children.

Ann affirmed the inventory in court on September 29, 1657.

## 1657 Family Division

The later division of the combined family estate identified Michael’s four surviving children and assigned them portions:

| Child | Recorded portion |
|---|---:|
| Jonathan Hopkinson | £25 |
| Jeremiah Hopkinson | £18 |
| John Hopkinson | £18 |
| Caleb Hopkinson | Approximately £18 |

This court record is important because it directly distinguishes Michael’s four children from John Trumble’s children by his two marriages.

## Name Variations

Michael’s name appears under several spellings:

- Michael Hopkinson
- Michaell Hopkinson
- Mighill Hobkinson

“Mighill” was a phonetic spelling of Michael. “Hobkinson” represents normal seventeenth-century variation in surname spelling. These forms refer to the same person.

The alternative spellings should be included in future record searches.

### Strong inference

- Michael was born in England.
- He was born around 1600.
- He worked as a weaver or textile producer.
- He also operated a farm.
