Michael Hopkinson was documented in Boston by January 1638/39, when the First Church recorded him as a servant of Jacob Elyott. In November 1639 the church dismissed Michael and three other members to the gathering of a church at the new settlement of Rowley. He subsequently held a Rowley house lot and became a Massachusetts Bay freeman on May 13, 1640.[^church][^gage]

Michael and his wife Ann had five documented births at Rowley. Their first son Jonathan died in infancy, after which they reused the name for another son. Four sons—Jonathan, Jeremiah, John, and Caleb—survived Michael and were distinguished as his children in the 1657 division of the combined Hopkinson-Trumble household estate.[^rowley-vr][^court-division]

Michael's probate inventory recorded a house, barn, agricultural land, meadow, livestock, books, arms, silver, specialized weaving equipment, and a share in a boat. The looms and associated equipment strongly support identifying him as a weaver or textile producer, while his land, crops, livestock, and agricultural tools support farming as another important part of his livelihood.[^probate]

## Family and relationships

### Parents and origins

Michael's parents and exact birthplace have not been established. His probable English birth is an inference from his appearance within the first generation of Massachusetts Bay settlement, not a proved parish identification.[^pope][^research-note]

A Kildwick, Yorkshire, parish entry is reported for the marriage of a Michael Hopkinson and Anna Brigge on December 2, 1624. Online trees often identify that groom with the later Rowley settler and consequently assign the Rowley man parents named Daniel Hopkinson and Susan Emot. No record examined in this project connects the Kildwick groom directly to Boston or Rowley.[^kildwick]

The proposed identification also conflicts with the age of Michael's Massachusetts widow. Ann reportedly testified on March 30, 1675/76 that she was about sixty, implying birth around 1615/16 and making her only about eight or nine in December 1624. The conflict could theoretically reflect two different Michaels, two wives both named Ann, or a substantially inaccurate reported age. No evidence presently establishes a second wife. The project therefore treats the Kildwick identification and proposed parents as unverified.[^pope][^kildwick]

### Wife

Michael's documented wife was [[people/Ann (Gott) Hopkinson|Ann (Gott) Hopkinson]]. **Gott is treated here as her probable maiden surname by strong inference, not as a confirmed vital-record fact.** Her 1678 will left Caleb Hopkinson a chest described in the published probate transcription as one that “father Gott made.” In this context, “father Gott” most naturally indicates a familial Gott connection: Caleb's biological father was Michael Hopkinson, and his later stepfathers were John Trumble and Richard Swan. Although an honorific or another non-parental relationship cannot be absolutely excluded, Congregational ministers in seventeenth-century Massachusetts were not ordinarily styled “Father,” making that explanation less likely.[^ann-probate]

The will does not identify “father Gott” more precisely or expressly state Ann's maiden name. Accordingly, the page title follows the project's working conclusion while preserving the evidentiary limitation. The frequently repeated claim that Ann was a daughter of the immigrant Charles Gott and Gift Palmer is not accepted: their documented 1625 marriage and known children do not fit Ann's reported birth around 1615/16. Her exact parentage remains unresolved.[^ann-probate][^pope][^gott]

After Michael's death, Ann married John Trumble in August 1650 and later Richard Swan on March 1, 1658/59. Her later estate and the 1657 court proceedings preserve the connection to her Hopkinson sons.[^pope][^court-division][^ann-probate]

The published Rowley vital records document both sons named Jonathan. The first was born January 12 and buried January 20, 1641/42. Reusing the name after an infant's death was a recognized early New England naming practice; here, however, the principal point is established directly by the two separate Rowley entries.[^rowley-vr]

Some online trees add older English children or identify Ann Wood, Mary Grant, and Susanna Todd as Michael's daughters. The 1657 court division instead described four surviving children of “Mighill Hobkinson”—Jonathan, Jeremiah, John, and Caleb—separately from John Trumble's children. This does not exclude a child who died before 1657, but it weighs strongly against adding other living daughters to Michael's family without new evidence.[^court-division][^research-note]

## Boston and removal to Rowley

The First Church in Boston provides Michael's earliest securely identified record. Under the heading for the sixth day of the eleventh month of 1638, the church listed “Michaell Hopkinson servant to our brother Jacob Elyott.” Under the colonial calendar, that date converts to January 6, 1638/39. “Servant” describes his relationship to Jacob Elyott at that moment, but the record does not specify Michael's work or the terms of his service.[^church]

On the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month 1639—November 24—the church dismissed Michael, Henry Sandys, William Stickney, and Richard Swanne “to the gathering of a Church at Rowley.” This establishes movement from Boston into the Rowley congregation. It does not establish Michael's immigration date, ship, English hometown, or participation in the original voyage associated with the Reverend Ezekiel Rogers.[^church][^gage]

## Life at Rowley

Published transcriptions of the early Rowley records place Michael among those receiving house lots. His lot contained approximately 1½ acres. He became a freeman on May 13, 1640, giving him formal political standing within Massachusetts Bay. The freeman record does not itself establish that he held a local office, and no identifying record examined here names him as selectman, constable, deputy, or militia officer.[^gage][^pope]

The surviving records use several spellings of his name, including **Michael Hopkinson**, **Michaell Hopkinson**, and **Mighill Hobkinson**. These variants are consistent with seventeenth-century phonetic and nonstandardized spelling and refer to the same Rowley householder in the connected church, family, and estate records.[^church][^probate][^court-division]

## Occupation and economic life

No record examined here explicitly assigns Michael an occupational title such as “weaver.” His inventory nevertheless listed a pair of looms, a shuttle, a temple, warping equipment, reeds, slays, wheels, cotton wool, yarn, and cloth. Together these specialized tools form strong circumstantial evidence that weaving or textile production was an important part of his livelihood.[^probate]

The inventory also recorded cultivated and uncultivated land, meadow, common rights, grain, livestock, scythes, axes, saws, and other tools. This supports farming as another significant economic activity. The page therefore lists **weaver** and **farmer** as strong occupational inferences rather than explicit record labels.[^probate]

## Property, probate, and estate

Michael's inventory totaled **£116 19s. 8d.** It included his house and barn; garden, orchard, yards, and adjoining swamp; 2½ acres of broken land at Batchelder Plain; five acres in the Bradford lots; eight acres of meadow and one acre of upland at the farm; common rights; cattle; household goods; textile equipment; books; silver; armor and powder; tools; and “part of the boat,” valued at £10 9s. The inventory does not identify the vessel's type or Michael's use of it.[^probate]

The inventory heading says it was taken “10:1:1648,” while its closing attestation bears “10:1:1649.” Because Michael was buried on February 28, 1648/49 and the inventory follows his death, the 1648 heading is likely a copying or dating inconsistency. The safest conclusion is that the inventory was made shortly after his burial in March 1649, without asserting a more precise modern date than the record securely permits.[^probate]

Formal administration was delayed. In 1657 John Trumble, then Ann's husband, petitioned the General Court for remission of a fine imposed because Ann had not administered her former husband's estate. The petition stated that an inventory had been taken and the estate had not been alienated. Relief was granted on condition that the inventory be submitted and the county court dispose of the estate for Ann and her children. Ann testified to the inventory in September 1657.[^probate]

The later family division is especially valuable because it separates the children into three groups: four of Michael Hopkinson, five children John Trumble had before marrying Ann, and two children of John and Ann. Michael's sons received recorded portions: £25 to Jonathan and £18 each to Jeremiah, John, and Caleb.[^court-division]

A subsequent Rowley land division allocated 67 acres to Richard Swan “in right of Michael Hopkinson.” Because Swan later married Ann, this represents the continuation or realization of Michael's proprietary right through the widow's household. It does not prove that Michael personally occupied or cultivated all 67 acres during his lifetime.[^gage]

## Death and burial

Michael's exact death date and cause have not been identified. The Rowley vital-record transcription records his burial on February 28, 1648 under the old calendar, conventionally displayed as February 28, 1648/49. He therefore died no later than that date, probably at Rowley given his residence, burial, and estate designation, but the evidence does not justify converting the burial date into an exact death date.[^rowley-vr][^probate]

The published record identifies Rowley as the place of burial but does not provide a cemetery section, plot, or surviving gravestone. Those details remain unresolved.[^rowley-vr]

## Direct-line position

Michael is the earliest presently documented person in the published direct Hopkinson line. His parents and earlier ancestry have not been established.

**Direct-line successor:** [[people/Caleb Hopkinson Sr.|Caleb Hopkinson Sr.]], Michael and Ann's youngest documented child.

Michael died when Caleb was only days old. The direct surname line continues through Caleb, but it should not be extended into a proposed English family until evidence identifies Michael's parents or connects him securely to an English parish household.

## Evidence assessment

### Confirmed

- Michael was recorded in the First Church in Boston in January 1638/39 as a servant of Jacob Elyott.[^church]
- The Boston church dismissed him to the gathering of a church at Rowley in November 1639.[^church]
- He held a Rowley house lot and became a Massachusetts Bay freeman on May 13, 1640.[^gage][^pope]
- His wife was named Ann.[^rowley-vr][^probate]
- Five Rowley births are documented to Michael and Ann, including two sons named Jonathan; the first Jonathan died in infancy.[^rowley-vr]
- Jonathan, Jeremiah, John, and Caleb survived Michael and were identified as his children in the 1657 family division.[^court-division]
- He owned real property, livestock, textile equipment, books, arms, silver, tools, household goods, and a share in a boat.[^probate]
- He was buried at Rowley on February 28, 1648/49.[^rowley-vr]

### Strong inference

- Michael was probably born in England around 1600, although no identifying birth or baptism has been established.[^pope][^research-note]
- His specialized loom equipment indicates that he probably worked as a weaver or textile producer.[^probate]
- His land, crops, livestock, and agricultural equipment indicate that farming was another significant livelihood.[^probate]
- He probably died at Rowley shortly before his recorded burial there.[^rowley-vr][^probate]
- Gott was probably Ann's maiden surname. The principal evidence is her 1678 bequest to Caleb of a chest that “father Gott made,” considered together with the identities of Caleb's father and stepfathers.[^ann-probate]

### Unverified

- Michael's alleged birth in Yorkshire in April 1601.[^kildwick]
- The identification of his parents as Daniel Hopkinson and Susan Emot.[^kildwick]
- The identification of the Rowley settler with the Michael Hopkinson who married Anna Brigge at Kildwick on December 2, 1624.[^kildwick]
- Claims that Michael had older English children or surviving daughters named Ann Wood, Mary Grant, or Susanna Todd.[^court-division][^research-note]
- Direct immigration with the Reverend Ezekiel Rogers party or arrival aboard any particular ship.[^gage]

### Unresolved

- Michael's exact birth date, birthplace, parents, and English household.
- His immigration date, ship, port of departure, and port of arrival.
- Ann's exact Gott relationship, parentage, and English origins; although Gott is the project's probable maiden-name conclusion, the will does not expressly state the relationship.
- The nature and duration of Michael's service to Jacob Elyott.
- Michael's exact death date and cause of death.
- The location of his burial within Rowley and whether a grave marker ever existed.
- The type and use of the boat in which the estate held a share.

## Research priorities

1. Search English parish, probate, manorial, apprenticeship, and textile records for a Michael Hopkinson who disappears before 1638 and whose family structure matches the Rowley evidence.
2. Test the Kildwick Michael Hopkinson–Anna Brigge couple through their complete parish register, children's baptisms and burials, probate, and evidence of continued English residence after 1638.
3. Locate any original immigration, port, employment, account, or court record connecting Michael with Jacob Elyott before or after the January 1638/39 church entry.
4. Examine the original Rowley town and church books behind the published vital-record and land transcriptions.
5. Obtain and compare the original Essex County probate file, General Court petition, and quarterly-court estate division with the published transcriptions.
6. Investigate the boat share and Michael's neighbors for possible textile, fishing, transport, or trading associations.
7. Search for a burial-ground record, plot evidence, or early monument transcription beyond the town burial entry.
8. Examine the original manuscript of Ann's 1678 will, Essex County probate docket 26,876, to verify the published reading “father Gott” and determine whether handwriting or context supplies a more precise relationship.
9. Search contemporary Gott probate, church, land, and court records for a man old enough to have been Ann's father or stepfather and for references to Ann under the surnames Hopkinson, Trumble, or Swan. Do not attach her to Charles Gott and Gift Palmer without evidence overcoming the chronological conflict.
